Q1. A manufacturer of exempt and taxable goods wants to claim a refund of accumulated ITC due to inverted duty structure. Walk through the eligibility conditions, formula, and documentary requirements under Rule 89(5) read with Section 54(3).
Navigating the refund process for an inverted duty structure—where the tax rate on inputs is higher than the tax rate on finished outputs—requires a precise application of Rule 89(5) of the CGST Rules.Here is the breakdown of the eligibility, the calculation, and the necessary documentation.1. Eligibility ConditionsUnder Section 54(3) of the CGST Act, a registered person may claim a refund of unutilized Input Tax Credit (ITC) at the end of any tax period if:Inverted Rate Structure: The credit has accumulated because the rate of tax on inputs is higher than the rate of tax on output supplies (other than nil rated or fully exempt supplies).Exclusion of Input Services: Refund is only available for ITC accumulated on inputs (goods). ITC on input services and capital goods is excluded from the calculation for inverted duty refunds.Exceptions: No refund is allowed if:The output supplies are nil-rated or fully exempt.The goods are exported out of India and are subject to export duty.The supplier claims a refund of output tax paid under the IGST Act.The specific goods are notified by the Government as ineligible for such refunds (e.g., construction services, solar modules, etc.).2. The Refund Formula [Rule 89(5)]The maximum refund amount is determined by a specific formula designed to neutralize the tax "trapped" by the inversion:$$Maximum\ Refund = \left( \frac{Net\ ITC \times Turnover\ of\ Inverted\ Rated\ Supply}{Adjusted\ Total\ Turnover} \right) - Tax\ Payable\ on\ Inverted\ Rated\ Supply \times \left( \frac{Net\ ITC}{ITC\ on\ Inputs + ITC\ on\ Input\ Services} \right)$$Definition of Variables:Net ITC: ITC availed on inputs during the relevant period (excluding ITC on input services and capital goods).Adjusted Total Turnover: Sum of taxable supplies (excluding exempt supplies) and zero-rated supplies during the period.Tax Payable on Inverted Rated Supply: The actual tax liability paid on the inverted rated goods through the electronic cash and credit ledgers.Note: The second part of the formula was amended to ensure that the "tax payable" is reduced proportionately between inputs and input services, preventing a mathematical disadvantage to the taxpayer.3. Documentary RequirementsTo file the refund application in Form GST RFD-01, the manufacturer must provide:Document TypePurposeStatement 1AA detailed statement containing invoice-level data of both inward (inputs) and outward (inverted) supplies.Statement 1A summary of the turnover and the calculated maximum refund amount as per the formula.Declaration/UndertakingA declaration that the incidence of tax has not been passed on to any other person (unjust enrichment), though this is generally deemed satisfied for inverted duty.GSTR-2B ReconciliationEnsure that all input invoices for which refund is claimed are reflected in the GSTR-2B of the relevant period.Copy of InvoicesWhile not always mandatory for the initial digital upload, keep copies of input and output invoices ready for the jurisdictional officer if a SCN (Show Cause Notice) is issued.4. Key Procedural StepsForm RFD-01: File electronically on the GST Portal.Credit Ledger Debit: Upon filing, the amount claimed as a refund will be automatically debited from your Electronic Credit Ledger.Acknowledgement (RFD-02): If the application is complete, an acknowledgment is issued within 15 days.Order (RFD-06): The proper officer will issue a final sanction order within 60 days of the date of the complete application.

Q2. A principal manufacturer sends goods to a job worker under a challan. If the goods are not returned within 3 years, what are the ITC consequences? Does the timeline differ for moulds/dies/jigs/fixtures vs. inputs?
In the context of Indian GST law (specifically Section 143 of the CGST Act), the movement of goods to a job worker is treated as a temporary transfer. However, if those goods do not return to the principal's place of business within a specific timeframe, the law creates a "deemed supply" fiction with significant Input Tax Credit (ITC) consequences.1. The "Deemed Supply" ConsequenceIf the goods sent to a job worker are not received back (or supplied directly from the job worker's premises) within the prescribed period, the following happens:Date of Supply: It is deemed that the principal supplied those goods to the job worker on the day the goods were originally sent out.Tax Liability: The principal must pay GST on that "deemed supply." You aren't technically "reversing" the original ITC, but you are creating a new tax liability that often outweighs the credit's benefit due to the accumulated interest.2. Q8. A Tier 4 Urban Co-operative Bank (UCB) wants to offer gold loans to a jewellery manufacturer. What are the RBI restrictions on (a) loan-to-value ratio, (b) end-use monitoring, and (c) aggregate exposure limits specific to a Tier 4 UCB?
As of 2026, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has introduced specific updates to the Lending Against Gold and Silver Collateral Directions, which directly impact how Tier 4 Urban Co-operative Banks (UCBs) manage facilities for commercial entities like jewellery manufacturers.

The regulatory framework for a Tier 4 UCB in this scenario is outlined below:

(a) Loan-to-Value (LTV) Ratio
Effective April 1, 2026, the RBI has transitioned from a flat LTV to a tiered structure based on the loan amount. For a Tier 4 UCB extending a loan to a manufacturer:

Tiered Limits:

Up to ₹2.5 lakh: 85% LTV

Above ₹2.5 lakh to ₹5 lakh: 80% LTV

Above ₹5 lakh: 75% LTV

Ongoing Maintenance: Unlike previous years where LTV was often checked only at sanction, banks must now ensure these LTV limits are maintained throughout the loan tenure. Any breach (e.g., due to a drop in gold prices) that persists for more than 30 consecutive days triggers an additional 1% provisioning requirement.

Valuation: Gold must be valued at the lower of the 30-day average closing price or the previous day's closing price as published by IBJA or SEBI-regulated exchanges.

(b) End-Use Monitoring
Because the borrower is a jewellery manufacturer (an "Income Generating Loan"), the monitoring requirements are more stringent than for a personal consumption loan:

Purpose Restriction: The bank is strictly prohibited from lending against gold if the proceeds are used to purchase primary gold, bullion, or gold coins. The loan must be for "need-based working capital" or manufacturing activities.

Documentary Evidence: For income-generating loans, the bank must collect and record documentary evidence (e.g., invoices, stock statements) to verify that the funds are being used for productive business purposes.

Credit Assessment: For loans exceeding ₹2.5 lakh, the bank must perform a detailed credit appraisal of the manufacturer’s repayment capacity and cash flows, rather than relying solely on the value of the pledged gold.

(c) Aggregate Exposure Limits
Tier 4 UCBs are governed by the Prudential Exposure Norms and specific ceilings for the sector:

Individual/Group Exposure: For a Tier 4 UCB, the individual borrower limit is typically 15% of Tier-I Capital (for a single borrower) and 25% of Tier-I Capital (for a group).

Small Value Loan Portfolio: Under the 2026 mandates, UCBs must ensure that at least 50% of their aggregate loan portfolio consists of "Small Value Loans" (loans up to ₹25 lakh or 0.4% of Tier-I capital). If the manufacturer’s loan exceeds this, it must fit within the remaining 50% of the bank's total advances.

Unsecured Limit: If any portion of the loan is not fully covered by the "realizable value" of the gold (e.g., due to market volatility), it counts toward the bank's aggregate unsecured exposure ceiling, which is generally 10% of total assets (though proposed to rise to 20% in late 2026).

Q11. A GTA (Goods Transport Agency) has a long-term contract at a fixed rate. Mid-contract, the GST rate on GTA services is revised. Who bears the additional GST – the transporter or the recipient? What does the contract’s force majeure / change-in-law clause typically need to say?
In the world of logistics and taxation, a mid-contract rate change can be a significant "who pays?" headache. Whether the transporter (GTA) or the recipient (the client) bears the additional GST depends almost entirely on the specific wording of your contract and the underlying tax laws of the jurisdiction.Here is how this usually breaks down:1. Who Bears the Burden?In most GST regimes, the incidence of tax is intended to fall on the final consumer or recipient. However, in a fixed-rate contract, the "legal" burden depends on how the price is defined:Inclusive of Taxes: If the contract states the rate is "$1,000 inclusive of all taxes," the transporter generally bears the burden. The GTA must absorb the tax hike into their profit margin because the total price paid by the recipient cannot change.Exclusive of Taxes: If the contract says "$1,000 + applicable GST," the recipient bears the burden. The base rate stays the same, and the GTA simply adds the new, higher tax rate to the invoice.Silence on Taxes: If the contract is silent, many jurisdictions (like India under Section 64A of the Sale of Goods Act) provide a default rule: unless a different intention appears in the contract, the seller/service provider can add the increased tax to the price and recover it from the buyer.2. The "Change in Law" ClauseThis is the most critical clause for long-term contracts. To protect both parties, a robust Change in Law clause should specifically address tax revisions. Typically, it needs to state:Definition: Explicitly include "changes in rates of tax, duties, or levies" within the definition of a Change in Law.Adjustment Mechanism: State that the contract price shall be "equitably adjusted" to reflect the increase or decrease in costs resulting from such change.Effective Date: Clarify that the adjustment applies only to services rendered on or after the official notification date of the tax change.Documentation: Require the GTA to provide proof of the legislative change before the recipient is obligated to pay the higher amount.Note: A Force Majeure clause is generally not the right tool for tax changes. Force Majeure covers "Acts of God" (floods, wars) that prevent performance. Since a tax hike doesn't physically stop the trucks from moving, it rarely triggers Force Majeure unless specifically written to include "Governmental Action" that makes the contract commercially unviable.3. Practical Steps for the GTA and RecipientIf you find yourself in this position mid-contract, consider these steps:StakeholderAction ItemTransporter (GTA)Issue a formal notice citing the government notification. If the contract is "inclusive," try to negotiate a "Supplementary Agreement" based on hardship.RecipientCheck if the GTA is under the Forward Charge (GTA pays) or Reverse Charge (Recipient pays) mechanism, as this determines who actually files the paperwork with the government.Both PartiesReview the "Price Escalation" clause. Some contracts allow for price tweaks based on fuel or inflation, which can be a secondary avenue for negotiation.Pro-Tip: The Anti-Profiteering AngleIn many GST systems, if the tax rate decreases, the law mandates that the benefit must be passed on to the recipient. A good contract should be "bi-directional"—protecting the GTA when taxes go up, and protecting the recipient when they go down.

Q12. A hotel collects an advance for a banquet booking when the GST rate is 18%. Before the event date, the rate is reduced to 12%. Which rate applies – the rate at the time of advance or the rate at the time of supply? Cite the relevant provision and any clarificatory circular.
In GST, when there is a change in the applicable tax rate, the "Time of Supply" rules shift. Instead of the standard rules, you must look specifically at Section 14 of the CGST Act, 2017, which governs the "Change in rate of tax in respect of supply of goods or services."

To determine whether the 18% or 12% rate applies, we look at three events:

Date of Issue of Invoice

Date of Receipt of Payment (the advance)

Date of Provision of Service (the banquet event)

The Applicable Rate: 12%
In your scenario, the service (the banquet) is provided after the rate change. Since two out of the three critical events—the provision of service and (presumably) the final invoice—occur after the rate reduction, the new rate of 12% applies.

Legal Provision: Section 14(b)(ii)
Under Section 14 of the CGST Act, if the goods or services have been supplied after the change in the rate of tax:

The Scenario: Advance received before the change, but the invoice is issued after the change.

The Rule: The rate of tax shall be the rate of tax as in force at the time of issue of invoice.

Therefore, since the banquet service is provided at the 12% rate period and the final invoice is issued then, the 12% rate prevails over the 18% rate that existed when the advance was taken.

Relevant Clarificatory Circular
The CBIC issued Circular No. 164/20/2021-GST (though primarily addressing restaurant/cloud kitchen services, it reinforces the principle of "Time of Supply"). More specifically, the fundamental mechanics of Section 14 are explained in the GST Flyer on Time of Supply issued by the CBIC.

Under the GST regime (Section 103 of the CGST Act), an advance ruling provides a "safe harbor" for the specific applicant, but its protection is not absolute. If the department successfully reclassifies your product, the ability to demand tax for past years depends heavily on whether the original ruling is still considered valid and whether there was any "suppression of facts."1. Can the Department Demand Differential Tax for Past Years?The General Rule: No (for the period the ruling was valid)As long as the facts and law remain unchanged, an advance ruling is binding on the jurisdictional officer in respect of the applicant. If a higher court (High Court or Supreme Court) or a legislative amendment changes the HSN classification for that product, the ruling loses its binding effect from the date of that change.Ruling Declared Void Ab Initio: Under Section 104, if the department proves the ruling was obtained by fraud, suppression of material facts, or misrepresentation, they can declare the ruling void from the beginning. In this scenario, it is as if the ruling never existed, and the department can demand differential tax, interest, and penalties for the entire past period (subject to limitation periods).New "Interpretation" vs. Change in Law: If the department simply changes its mind or a different officer disagrees with the AAR's logic (without a change in law or a finding of fraud), they typically cannot override the binding nature of the ruling for the past period for that specific applicant.2. Limits of an Advance Ruling’s Binding EffectThe binding nature of a GST Advance Ruling is "in personam" (limited to the person), not "in rem" (applying to everyone).Limit CategoryScope of Binding EffectParties BoundOnly binding on the applicant and their jurisdictional officer. It does not bind the department regarding other taxpayers, even if they sell the exact same product.Territorial LimitA ruling from the AAR of one state (e.g., Maharashtra) is not binding on the tax authorities of another state (e.g., Karnataka), as held in Sarkar Diesel (2024).Judicial HierarchyAn AAR is a quasi-judicial body. Its rulings cannot override judgments of the High Court or Supreme Court. If a higher court issues a contrary ruling, the AAR ruling is effectively neutralized.DurationIt remains binding only so long as the "law, facts, or circumstances" remain the same. A change in the HSN explanatory notes or a notification changing the rate can terminate the ruling's effect.Summary for your situationIf the department is challenging your classification despite a favorable ruling:Check for "Suppression": Did you omit any technical detail about the product in your AAR application? If not, the Section 104 "Void Ab Initio" route is difficult for the department to take.Identify the Trigger: Is the department's challenge based on a new Supreme Court judgment or a change in the Law? If yes, you are protected only up until the date of that judgment/change.Third-Party Rulings: If you are relying on an advance ruling issued to a competitor or a different company, it has no binding effect on your case; the department is free to demand differential tax for all past years within the limitation period.

Q17. While filing GSTR-9 (annual return), a taxpayer finds that Table 8A (auto-populated ITC from GSTR-2A/2B) does not match their books. What are the possible reasons for mismatch, and what is the correct way to reconcile and report the difference in Table 8C?
Gemini said
Reconciling Table 8A of GSTR-9 is often the most challenging part of the annual return, as it represents the "system's view" of your Input Tax Credit (ITC) based on your suppliers’ filings.

Differences between your books and Table 8A are common. Here is a breakdown of why they happen and how to handle the reporting in Table 8C.

1. Common Reasons for Mismatch
If your books show more (or less) ITC than Table 8A, it usually stems from one of the following:

Supplier Non-Compliance: The supplier hasn't filed their GSTR-1, or they filed it but didn't pay the taxes (GSTR-3B).

Incorrect GSTIN/Filing: The supplier accidentally uploaded the invoice against the wrong GSTIN or marked it as B2C instead of B2B.

Cut-off Date Issues: Table 8A is "frozen" on a specific date (usually in November following the end of the financial year). If a supplier filed belatedly after this cutoff, the credit won't appear in 8A even if it appears in your GSTR-2B.

Ineligible ITC: Some entries in 8A might relate to "blocked credits" (Section 17(5)) which you correctly didn't claim in your books, creating a "positive" variance in 8A.

Place of Supply (PoS) Errors: If the supplier mentioned the wrong State Code, the ITC might be blocked for you but still show up in the auto-populated data.

2. Reconciling and Reporting in Table 8C
Table 8C is titled "ITC on inward supplies received during the financial year but availed in the next financial year up to specified period." To report correctly, follow these steps:

The Logic of Table 8C
This table is specifically for "In-transit" ITC. You use this to account for credit that belongs to the financial year being filed (Year A) but was actually claimed in the GSTR-3B of the following year (Year B).

How to Report
Identify the Value: Look at your GSTR-3B filings for April to November of the following financial year. Total the ITC that pertains to invoices dated within the current financial year.

Enter the Amount: Input this total into Table 8C.

The Goal: The formula for Table 8D is:

8D=8A−(8B+8C)
8B: ITC claimed in GSTR-3B during the current FY.

8C: ITC pertaining to the current FY but claimed in the next FY.

Handling the Mismatch
If 8A > (8B + 8C): You have "unutilized" credit. This usually happens if suppliers uploaded invoices you never claimed (e.g., missed invoices or ineligible credit). This will lapse.

If 8A < (8B + 8C): This is a red flag. It means you claimed more credit than what is reflected in the GST portal. You should have a robust reconciliation statement ready, as the department may issue a notice (ASMT-10) for this difference.

3. Best Practices for Table 8 Reconciliation
Download the 8A Document: Use the "Download Table 8A Document Details" excel utility from the GST portal. It provides a line-by-line break up of which supplier contributed to that figure.

Ignore Table 8A for "Claiming": Remember, Table 8A is for disclosure and reconciliation only. You cannot use GSTR-9 to claim new ITC that you missed in your monthly GSTR-3B filings.

Lapsing Credit: If there is ITC in 8A that you don't want to claim (or can't claim), ensure it is accounted for in Table 8E or 8F so that the "ITC to be lapsed" (Table 8K) is accurate.

While the GST Council has the sovereign power to change rates, the courts have increasingly balanced this power against the Doctrine of Promissory Estoppel.If a manufacturer invested capital based on a specific promise from the state (e.g., a "tax-free" status or a specific subsidy), the government cannot arbitrarily withdraw that benefit without an overriding public interest.1.

### GST 2.0 — Discounts under GST

**Executive Summary**

The 56th GST Council Meeting (3 September 2025) has recommended legislative amendments to Sections 15 and 34 of the CGST Act, 2017 to reform the treatment of post-sale discounts. The changes remove the requirement of pre-agreeing discounts at the time of supply and linking them to specific invoices. Post-sale discounts will now be routed through GST credit notes under an amended Section 34, with mandatory ITC reversal by the recipient, replacing the cumbersome certificate-based mechanism under Circular 212/6/2024-GST.

## Current Legal Position

**Section 15(3)(a) — Trade discounts on the invoice.** Where a discount is shown on the face of the invoice at the time of supply, it is excluded from the value of supply. No further conditions apply. This covers upfront discounts, cash discounts mentioned on the invoice, and quantity breaks reflected at the point of billing.

**Section 15(3)(b) — Post-sale discounts.** For any discount granted after the supply has been made, two cumulative conditions must be satisfied for it to be excluded from value:

- **Section 15(3)(b)(i):** The discount must be established in terms of an agreement entered into at or before the time of supply, and must be specifically linked to relevant invoices.
- **Section 15(3)(b)(ii):** The recipient must reverse the corresponding input tax credit attributable to the discount.

**Where Section 15(3)(b) conditions are not met.** The supplier may issue a financial or commercial credit note as an accounting entry. However, such a credit note does not reduce GST liability. The supplier continues to bear tax on the original invoice value, and the recipient's ITC remains unaffected.

**Circular No. 212/6/2024-GST (26 June 2024).** This circular introduced a mechanism requiring suppliers to obtain certificates from recipients confirming that ITC attributable to the discount had been reversed. The certificate was a condition precedent for the supplier to reduce output tax liability through a credit note.

**Practical difficulties under the current framework:**

- Dynamic discount schemes — volume-based incentives, year-end rebates, staggered performance targets — are rarely documented in a single agreement before the first invoice is raised. The Section 15(3)(b)(i) requirement of a pre-existing agreement and invoice-level linkage made it structurally impossible to claim many legitimate commercial discounts.
- In e-commerce and digital distribution channels, discount terms change daily. Mapping each discount to a specific invoice number is operationally impractical.
- The certificate mechanism under Circular 212 imposed a bilateral coordination burden. Suppliers had to chase recipients for signed certificates; recipients had no statutory obligation to cooperate.
- Assessments frequently resulted in demands disallowing credit notes where the agreement was found to be insufficiently specific or executed after the date of the earliest linked invoice.
- The FMCG, pharmaceutical, and automobile sectors — where multi-tier distribution and retrospective discount schemes are standard industry practice — faced persistent litigation on whether their discount structures met Section 15(3)(b)(i).

## Proposed Changes

**Omission of Section 15(3)(b)(i).** The Council has recommended omitting Section 15(3)(b)(i) of the CGST Act, thereby removing the requirement of establishing the discount through a prior agreement and linking it to specific invoices. After this amendment, neither a pre-supply agreement nor invoice-level mapping will be a condition for excluding post-sale discounts from the value of supply.

**Amendment to Section 34.** Section 34 of the CGST Act will be amended to include a specific reference to Section 15(3)(b). Credit notes issued under the amended Section 34 will now expressly cover post-sale discounts. Where a post-sale discount is given and the value of supply is reduced through a GST credit note, the recipient must reverse the corresponding ITC.

**Rescission of Circular 212/6/2024-GST.** The manual certificate-based mechanism requiring suppliers to obtain ITC reversal confirmations from recipients is being withdrawn. The circular will be rescinded in its entirety.

**Clarificatory circular on contentious issues.** The Council has recommended issuance of a clarificatory circular addressing three specific matters:

1. Non-reversal of ITC on account of post-sale discounts given through financial or commercial credit notes (i.e., credit notes that do not reduce GST liability).
2. Treatment of post-sale discounts provided by a manufacturer to a dealer as additional consideration in the dealer-to-end-customer transaction.
3. Treatment of post-sale discounts as consideration in lieu of promotional activities performed by the dealer.

## Action Items

1. Review existing discount schemes — volume incentives, year-end rebates, performance-linked payouts — and identify which currently fail the Section 15(3)(b)(i) conditions and are being routed through commercial credit notes.
2. Prepare to issue GST credit notes (not just commercial credit notes) for all post-sale discounts once the amended Section 34 is notified, so that output tax liability is reduced at the supplier end.
3. Update billing and ERP systems to handle credit note issuance under the amended Section 34 framework, including auto-population of discount amounts and tax adjustment entries.
4. Brief sales and procurement teams on the distinction between GST credit notes (which now reduce tax liability and require recipient ITC reversal) and financial/commercial credit notes (which do not).
5. Monitor issuance of the clarificatory circular on dealer incentives, promotional discounts, and the additional consideration question — these will determine how manufacturer-to-dealer schemes are restructured.
6. Assess IMS (Invoice Management System) readiness for automated ITC reversal tracking, since recipient-side ITC reversal is now the sole operative condition under the amended Section 15(3)(b).
7. Discontinue the Circular 212 certificate collection process once the rescission notification is issued; retain existing certificates as documentation for any open assessment periods.

## Practitioner Insights

**Why Section 15(3)(b)(i) removal matters.** The pre-agreement and invoice-linkage requirement was the single largest source of discount-related litigation in GST. FMCG companies operating multi-tier distribution with retrospective volume rebates, pharmaceutical firms offering trade incentives to stockists, and automobile manufacturers with dealer performance schemes all faced recurring demands. The amendment eliminates this structural mismatch between commercial practice and legal conditions.

**GST credit notes vs. financial/commercial credit notes.** This distinction remains critical post-amendment. A GST credit note under Section 34 reduces the supplier's output tax liability and triggers mandatory ITC reversal by the recipient. A financial or commercial credit note is an accounting adjustment with no GST consequence — the supplier's liability and the recipient's ITC remain unchanged. The forthcoming clarificatory circular is expected to confirm that financial credit notes do not require ITC reversal, settling a question that has been contested in assessments.

**IMS readiness and transition risk.** The amended framework places the entire compliance burden on the ITC reversal by the recipient. Until the IMS is fully operational for automated credit note matching and ITC reversal reconciliation, there will be a gap between the supplier's credit note issuance and the recipient's reversal. Suppliers should build internal tracking to verify that recipients have accepted credit notes on IMS and reversed ITC accordingly.

**Manufacturer-to-dealer discount structures.** The clarificatory circular will address whether a post-sale discount given by a manufacturer to a dealer constitutes "additional consideration" in the dealer's onward supply to the end customer. Under the current interpretation in certain advance rulings, such discounts have been treated as additional consideration, requiring the dealer to include the discount amount in the value of the onward supply. The Council's decision to issue specific clarification signals that this interpretation may be narrowed.

**Transacting vs. non-transacting party discounts.** Where a manufacturer grants a discount directly to a retailer or end consumer (bypassing the distributor in the supply chain), the discount flows to a non-transacting party. The circular may clarify whether such discounts qualify for value reduction under Section 15(3)(b) at all, or whether they are treated as separate promotional expenditure outside the supply chain.

**Promotional activity consideration.** The third limb of the clarificatory circular — post-sale discounts as consideration for promotional activities by the dealer — addresses the reverse charge and separate supply risk. If a discount is recharacterised as consideration for a service (display, shelf placement, marketing), it becomes a separate taxable supply by the dealer to the manufacturer. The circular's position on this will determine whether current industry practices of clubbing trade discounts with marketing incentives need to be unbundled.

**Overall direction.** The amendment represents a liberalisation of the discount provisions. By removing the pre-agreement condition and routing all post-sale discounts through the credit note mechanism with recipient ITC reversal as the sole condition, the framework aligns with commercial reality. The shift from bilateral certificate coordination to system-driven ITC reversal through IMS is consistent with the broader GST 2.0 direction of automated compliance and reduced manual intervention.

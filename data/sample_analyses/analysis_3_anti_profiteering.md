### GST 2.0 — Anti-Profiteering

**Executive Summary**

The 56th GST Council Meeting (3 September 2025) has approved a sweeping rate rationalization — collapsing GST into three slabs (5%, 18%, 40%) effective 22 September 2025. With the National Anti-Profiteering Authority having ceased new investigations from 1 April 2025, the onus to pass on rate reduction benefits now falls squarely on industry. Businesses face a narrow 19-day window to recalibrate pricing, and the absence of a statutory enforcement body does not eliminate legal exposure.

## Current Legal Position

**Section 171 of the CGST Act, 2017** mandates that any reduction in the rate of tax or the benefit of input tax credit shall be passed on to the recipient by way of commensurate reduction in prices. The provision remains on the statute book — it has not been repealed or amended. The obligation is self-executing: it binds every registered person irrespective of whether an enforcement authority exists.

**National Anti-Profiteering Authority (NAA)** was constituted under Section 171(2) to examine complaints of profiteering. Over its tenure, the NAA delivered over 500 orders but faced persistent criticism for lacking a uniform methodology. In FMCG cases, the Authority adopted a SKU-level base price comparison approach — computing the pre-reduction and post-reduction base prices for each stock-keeping unit and treating any shortfall in price reduction as profiteered amount. In real estate, it took a project-level approach. In services, it sometimes accepted company-wide assessments. This inconsistency left businesses without a defensible benchmark for compliance.

**Notification No. 19/2024-Central Tax dated 30.09.2024** directed that the NAA would not accept new applications for profiteering examination with effect from 01.04.2025. Pending cases were to be concluded by the Competition Commission of India (CCI), which had been designated as the successor body for residual matters under Rule 137 of the CGST Rules.

The practical effect: no active investigation body exists for profiteering complaints arising from the September 2025 rate changes. Section 171 remains law, but there is no authority currently empowered to initiate or adjudicate new complaints. This creates an enforcement vacuum — not a legal safe harbour.

## Proposed Changes

**Three-slab rate structure.** The Council has rationalized the existing four-tier structure (5%, 12%, 18%, 28%) into a simplified framework: a Merit Rate of 5%, a Standard Rate of 18%, and a de-merit rate of 40% for select goods. The 12% and 28% slabs are effectively eliminated for most goods and services. Pan masala, gutkha, cigarettes, chewing tobacco, zarda, unmanufactured tobacco, and bidi remain at existing rates until compensation cess loan obligations are fully discharged.

**Magnitude of rate reductions.** The press release lists over 30 categories of goods receiving rate cuts. Common household items (hair oil, soap, toothpaste, shampoo, bicycles, kitchenware) move from 18%/12% to 5%. Packaged food items (namkeens, sauces, pasta, noodles, chocolates, coffee, butter, ghee) move from 12%/18% to 5%. Cement drops from 28% to 18%. Individual life and health insurance policies become fully exempt. Drugs and medicines see reductions across the board, with 36 lifesaving drugs moving to NIL.

**Rate increases on select inputs.** The rationalization is not uniformly downward. Goods previously at 12% that do not qualify for the 5% merit rate will move to 18%. Goods Transport Agency (GTA) services at 12% with ITC will migrate to 18%. Coal, previously at 5% plus compensation cess, will attract 18% GST with cess abolition — the net incidence depends on whether the cess component exceeds the rate differential.

**Government expectation on passthrough.** The press release frames these changes as intended to benefit the common citizen. The rate reductions on household goods, food items, insurance, and medicines carry an implicit expectation of consumer-facing price reductions. While the press release does not explicitly invoke Section 171, the stated purpose — making goods and services "affordable for the common man" — signals that the government expects businesses to translate rate cuts into lower shelf prices.

**No new enforcement mechanism announced.** The Council did not recommend reconstitution of the NAA or creation of a successor anti-profiteering body. No new rules or amendments to Section 171 were proposed. The enforcement gap persists.

**Alternative enforcement routes remain available.** The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 empowers the Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) to issue directions against unfair trade practices, including failure to pass on benefits. The Legal Metrology Act, 2009 requires revised MRP declarations when tax rates change. State-level consumer forums and the CCPA can act on complaints of price gouging. These are blunter instruments than the NAA's specialized framework, but they carry their own penalties.

**Implementation date.** All rate changes on goods (except tobacco products) and services take effect from 22 September 2025.

## Action Items

1. **Map every HSN/SAC code** to its pre- and post-September rate. Build a comprehensive matrix covering all inputs, outputs, and exempt supplies. Complete this by 10 September 2025 to allow time for downstream pricing decisions.

2. **Conduct a holistic cost impact assessment** covering both input and output rate changes before 22 September 2025. Net benefit to the business is output rate reduction minus lost ITC (from newly exempt outputs) minus increased input costs (from items moving to higher slabs). Only the net benefit — not the gross output reduction — need be passed on.

3. **Document the methodology used for computing benefits passed on to consumers.** Select and formally adopt one approach — SKU-level, category-level, or company-wide — and record the rationale for that choice. The NAA's inconsistent precedent means no single method is legally mandated; defensibility depends on consistency and documentation.

4. **Maintain comprehensive audit trails.** Retain pricing decisions, cost computation working papers, board/management approvals, and communications with supply chain partners. If a profiteering framework is reintroduced retroactively or complaints are filed under consumer protection law, contemporaneous documentation is the primary defence.

5. **Update MRPs, price lists, packaging materials, and in-store displays** to reflect revised GST rates. The Legal Metrology Act requires MRP re-declaration when the tax component changes. Non-compliance with MRP requirements is a separate offence attracting penalties under the Legal Metrology Act independent of any anti-profiteering action.

6. **Review all contracts for GST passthrough and adjustment clauses.** Tax-inclusive contracts require immediate price adjustment. Tax-exclusive contracts with "GST as applicable" wording may not require revision, but the commercial impact on the counterparty must be assessed. Mixed arrangements need clause-by-clause analysis.

7. **Renegotiate vendor terms where input costs have changed.** GTA services moving from 12% to 18% increase logistics costs. Inputs moving from 12% to 18% where the buyer was on the margin of the composition scheme or exempt supplies create ITC availability questions. Proactive vendor engagement before 22 September prevents post-facto disputes.

8. **File transitional ITC claims** for any compensation cess credit balances that may become stranded if the cess is abolished without a utilization mechanism. Monitor CBIC notifications for transition provisions.

## Practitioner Insights

**The net benefit calculation is not straightforward.** A manufacturer whose output rate drops from 18% to 5% appears to receive a 13-percentage-point reduction. But if coal input costs increase (cess abolished, GST to 18%), GTA freight moves from 12% to 18%, and certain intermediate inputs also move up, the actual benefit available to pass on to consumers is materially lower. Firms that mechanically pass on the full output rate difference without accounting for input cost increases will absorb losses.

**Exempt outputs trigger ITC blocking under Section 17(2).** Where outputs become fully exempt — individual life insurance, individual health insurance — all attributable input tax credit is blocked. The embedded GST cost on inputs, capital goods, and input services becomes part of the cost base. This reduces the net benefit available for passthrough, potentially to zero or negative. Insurance companies must model this impact before repricing policies.

**Compensation cess credit accumulation is a live risk.** Businesses carrying accumulated compensation cess credit (common in automobiles, aerated beverages, and tobacco supply chains) face potential crystallization of that credit as a sunk cost if no transition mechanism is notified. The Council's decision to retain existing rates on tobacco products until cess obligations are discharged provides some clarity for that sector, but manufacturers of formerly cess-bearing goods that have moved to the new slab structure have no such assurance.

**Pre-September discounts complicate benefit computation.** Many FMCG companies run trade schemes, volume discounts, and promotional offers throughout the year. If a company has already reduced effective prices through a 10% trade discount prior to the rate change, the question arises: does that discount count toward the Section 171 obligation? The NAA's past orders were split on this — some treated pre-existing discounts as irrelevant, others acknowledged commercial pricing dynamics. Without active adjudication, the conservative position is to treat the Section 171 obligation as computed on the listed price before any commercial discounts.

**Legal Metrology compliance creates a parallel risk track.** The Legal Metrology (Packaged Commodities) Rules require MRP re-declaration within a specified timeframe when the tax incidence changes. Failure to revise MRPs can result in seizure of goods, penalties, and prosecution under the Legal Metrology Act. This is independent of anti-profiteering — a company could correctly pass on the benefit in its trade pricing but still face Legal Metrology action if packaging is not updated.

**International precedent offers methodological guidance.** Australia used a formula-based approach during its GST introduction (the "New Tax System Price Exploitation Code"), comparing pre-GST and post-GST prices with allowance for non-tax cost changes. Malaysia adopted a similar mechanism during its GST-to-SST transition. While India does not mandate a specific formula, adopting a documented methodology benchmarked to international practice strengthens the audit trail if enforcement resumes.

**The NAA sunset is not permanent insulation.** The government retains the power to reconstitute an anti-profiteering body through notification under Section 171(2) or through legislative amendment. Past practice shows India's willingness to introduce retrospective provisions. The 56th Council's silence on enforcement should be read as a deferral, not an abandonment. Businesses that treat the NAA sunset as a free pass face the risk of retrospective scrutiny if public sentiment on pricing triggers a policy response.

**The 19-day implementation window is operationally aggressive.** From 3 September (Council recommendation) to 22 September (effective date) leaves minimal time for ERP reconfiguration, contract renegotiation, packaging changes, and pricing decisions. Businesses should prioritize system readiness and provisional pricing, with a formal review and adjustment mechanism within 60 days of implementation.
